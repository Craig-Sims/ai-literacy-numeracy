Read: a pedagogically grounded approach to AI-supported literacy teaching

1. Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) systems can produce text and other content in response to instructions, offering educators new ways to prepare resources, model language choices and support feedback. In literacy education, however, the central question is not whether AI can generate a plausible text or response, but whether its use improves learners’ capacity to read critically and write effectively. UNESCO (2023) cautions against treating GenAI as a solution to education’s fundamental challenges and advocates a human-centred approach that protects learners and supports meaningful educational use. This paper outlines a pedagogically grounded approach to AI-supported literacy teaching, with examples relevant to Indonesian primary and secondary classrooms.

2. Opportunities for reading and writing instruction

In reading instruction, GenAI may help teachers create vocabulary previews, generate questions at different levels of complexity, provide alternative explanations of difficult passages and prepare discussion prompts. Used carefully, these functions can support access to texts and allow teachers to respond to varied learner needs. For example, a Grade 5 class reading an informational passage about mangrove forests in coastal Indonesia could use teacher-generated questions addressing retrieval, vocabulary in context, inference and evidence-based interpretation. The teacher should verify that proposed answers are supported by the passage and should ask learners to identify the textual evidence behind their responses. This maintains the distinction between making a text more accessible and outsourcing the interpretive work of reading.

In writing instruction, GenAI can support the process of planning, drafting, feedback, revision and reflection. Rather than asking a system to produce a finished assignment, teachers can prompt it to act as a writing coach: identifying a strong idea, pointing out where reasoning needs evidence, suggesting a cohesion issue and posing a question for the writer to consider. In a Grade 8 persuasive-writing task about reducing single-use plastics at school, students might decide which feedback to accept, revise their own paragraphs and explain the reasoning behind one change. This approach makes writing decisions visible and foregrounds the learner’s agency. The teacher remains responsible for checking the quality and appropriateness of AI-generated feedback.

3. A human-centred and inclusive pedagogy

AI-supported literacy activities should begin with explicit learning intentions and criteria for success. Teachers need to determine which aspects of a task require direct instruction, modelling, independent practice, dialogue or feedback, and then consider whether AI offers a clear pedagogical benefit. UNESCO’s AI Competency Framework for Teachers emphasises human-centred thinking, ethics, foundations and applications of AI, AI pedagogy, and AI for professional learning (Miao & Cukurova, 2024). These dimensions suggest that teachers require more than operational skill: they need the judgement to decide when AI use is appropriate, how to evaluate outputs and how to maintain accountability for learning.

Inclusive design is also important. AI may assist teachers in preparing glossaries, sentence frames, chunked texts or alternative explanations, including supports for multilingual learners. Yet generated translations and adaptations can distort meaning, privilege dominant language varieties or misrepresent local cultural knowledge. CAST’s Universal Design for Learning Guidelines 3.0 encourage educators to anticipate barriers, value diverse identities and languages, and provide multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression (CAST, 2024). AI can contribute to such flexibility, but it does not guarantee inclusion. Teachers should review materials in context and ensure that students without reliable access to devices or connectivity can participate meaningfully.

4. Risks, assessment and responsible use

GenAI can produce fluent but inaccurate explanations, fabricated references, biased representations and content that does not fit the learners’ age or context. UNESCO (2023) therefore stresses data protection, age-appropriate use and ethical validation. Schools and teachers should avoid entering identifiable student information or confidential records into tools unless an approved and secure process explicitly permits it. Outputs should be checked against trusted sources and original texts; learners should be taught to question claims, identify uncertainty and acknowledge AI assistance according to school policy.

Assessment practices also need to make learning visible. A polished final product may not reliably show what a student can independently understand or compose if the extent of AI assistance is unknown. Teachers can gather evidence through annotations, planning notes, drafts, revision histories, oral explanations and reflective statements alongside the final reading response or written text. The OECD (2023) similarly highlights the importance of trustworthy, useful, effective and equitable digital education ecosystems. In literacy classrooms, this means evaluating not only the quality of the output, but also the learner’s reasoning, decision-making and development.

5. Implications for teacher professional learning

Professional learning should combine conceptual understanding, hands-on experimentation and critical reflection. A practical workshop can ask teachers to select an existing reading or writing task, identify its literacy objective, test a carefully specified prompt, examine the output for accuracy and bias, and redesign the activity so learners still need to interpret, compose, justify and reflect. A useful prompt specifies the learner group, context, learning goal, source material, expected output and quality checks. For instance, teachers can instruct an AI tool to create comprehension questions using only a supplied passage and to provide evidence-based rationales, while explicitly prohibiting unsupported additions.

Implementation should begin with a small, low-risk pilot rather than system-wide adoption. Teachers can compare learner work, participation, feedback quality and workload with their previous approach, and seek student and colleague perspectives. The key evaluation question is whether AI improves the quality of literacy learning, not simply whether it makes resource production faster. Professional learning should therefore develop teachers’ capacity to make informed choices, document their decisions and refine practice in response to evidence.

6. A Literacy framework – The Strathclyde Three Domain Tool

The three domains of literacy learning developed by Professor Sue Ellis from the University of Strathclyde are designed to provide a comprehensive framework for understanding and teaching literacy. These domains are:

Cognitive Knowledge and Skills

This domain focuses on the traditional aspects of literacy, such as:

  • Phonological Awareness: Understanding the sound structure of language.
  • Phonics: The relationship between letters and sounds.
  • Fluency: The ability to read with speed, accuracy, and proper expression.
  • Comprehension: Understanding and interpreting what is read.
  • Writing Skills: The ability to express thoughts and ideas in written form.

Personal and Social Identity

This domain emphasises the role of literacy in shaping an individual's identity and social interactions:

  • Cultural and Social Capital: The knowledge, skills, and experiences that individuals bring from their cultural and social backgrounds.
  • Personal Identity: How literacy contributes to a person's sense of self and their ability to express their identity.
  • Social Identity: How literacy enables individuals to participate in and contribute to their communities.

Cultural and Social Practices

This domain considers literacy as a social practice that is influenced by cultural and social contexts:

  • Literacy as a Social Practice: Understanding that reading and writing are not just cognitive skills but are embedded in social and cultural contexts.
  • Context-Embedded Learning: Recognizing that literacy practices vary across different social settings and are influenced by ideologies and policies.
  • Integrated Approaches: Encouraging teaching methods that integrate cognitive skills with social and cultural understanding

These domains highlight the importance of considering the whole child in literacy education, recognizing that literacy development is not just about acquiring skills but also about personal growth and social participation.

7. Research overview
  • Writing feedback and revision: A small Indonesian EFL case study examined ChatGPT alongside Grammarly and QuillBot. The tools could help students engage with feedback, but whether feedback was actually processed and used depended partly on students’ choices and support. The authors frame AI as a complement to teaching—not a replacement—particularly because tools may emphasise surface correction over ideas and organisation. Gozali et al. (2024)
  • Adaptive reading support: In adult literacy classes, an intelligent tutoring system was used alongside teacher-led instruction in a 100-hour intervention. Learners showed different interaction patterns and comprehension gains; gains were highest for “conscientious readers” and lowest for learners who were slow and inaccurate. This suggests adaptive tools may help tailor practice, but also that a single approach will not suit every reader. It is evidence about a structured tutoring system, not a general proof that chatbots improve reading. Fang et al. (2022)
  • Literacy teaching and classroom rules: A study of 27 preservice secondary English teachers analysed classroom AI policies around writing, feedback, reading, and research. It illustrates teachers’ efforts to define appropriate AI use, but does not test whether those policies or AI use improve student literacy outcomes. Nash & Burriss (2026)

Ask for a model response

Identify a writing task you would ask your students to complete. Ask an AI tool of your choice to create a model response for this task.

An example prompt might be

“Students are asked to write a short description of a garden. Write a model response using descriptive language at an appropriate level for a 10 year old Indonesian student.”
