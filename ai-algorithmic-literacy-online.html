Name one algorithm that shapes your day

What you will be able to do

  • Explain the three dimensions of algorithmic literacy: cognitive, affective, and behaviour-related
  • Explain how algorithmic systems including AI tools produce their outputs and what they are optimised for, including their capabilities and limitations
  • Critically evaluate whose interests an algorithmic system serves, and how it might shape what you see, know and decide in your own teaching/ mentoring.
  • Apply strategies to shape AI output in your own context, such as prompting a tool to challenge rather than agree.

This is a 2 minute reflection. Name one algorithm that shapes your day (a recommendation feed, a maps app) and one time its output was unhelpful or surprising.

A short quiz: where are you now?

This quiz looks at three dimensions of algorithmic literacy: knowing (cognitive), evaluating (affective) and acting (behavioural). Choose the answer closest to what you would do. After each answer you will see a short note.

Cognitive dimension

1. You search for a teaching resource and the top results are all very similar. What is your first thought?

2. A colleague says, “The chatbot knows the answer. It just looked it up.” What is your response?

Affective dimension

3. You ask a chatbot to review your lesson idea and it replies, “Excellent idea!” What do you do?

4. Your institution recommends a free AI app for PPG students. What do you ask?

Behavior-related dimension

5. When did you last change how you use an algorithmic tool because of something you learned about it?

6. A chatbot keeps agreeing with you. What do you do?

Concept 1: Does the tool agree too easily?

Test an AI tool

Pick a topic from your own teaching or mentoring and open an AI tool of your choice. Write a short draft idea of your own, such as a lesson outline or activity in three or four sentences, with no student data. Ask the tool for feedback on it. Note how it responds. Is it mostly praise?

How did the tool respond to my idea?

Run at least two of the probes below.

  • Push back: reply “Are you sure?” or “I think that’s wrong” to something it said that was right. Does it hold its position or reverse?
  • Fish for agreement: state a questionable claim about teaching as if you believe it. Does it correct you or go along?
  • Ask for the weakest points: “What are the three biggest weaknesses of my idea?” Compare this with the first response.
  • Same prompt again: what stays the same, and what changes?

The probes I ran

Add an instruction asking it to challenge you rather than agree, then repeat.

For example

“Act as a critical colleague. Tell me where this idea is weak before saying what is strong.”

  • What AI does well, and under what conditions (e.g. drafting, reformatting, generating options/ examples/ scenarios). Conditions: clear prompts, real contexts, critical review by the teacher. AI is a scaffold, not a replacement for professional judgement.
  • The limitations of AI
  • Strategies for addressing some of the limitations
