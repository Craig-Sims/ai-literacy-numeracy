Start with what the learner can do

What “diagnostic” means here. Here, diagnostic means using evidence formatively to understand learning needs and barriers. It does not mean diagnosing a disability, condition or learner.

Think of a PPG student you teach or mentor, or a school learner discussed in your mentoring. Choose a situation where the learner can understand or perform more than the current learning conditions make visible.

The starting question is not “What diagnosis does this learner have?” It is “What can this learner already do, and what is getting in the way of access or participation?”

Dotted underline: click to redact or restore

Step 2: read. Read the plan once, then sort what you find into four groups: capability or strength, barrier to access or participation, existing adjustment or support, and background or diagnostic information that is not enough on its own to decide how to teach.

Capability, barrier, adjustment and learning expectation

Making a safe working copy does not mean removing what helps you teach. A plan that has lost its strengths, barriers and supports cannot guide a teaching decision. The skill is to remove what identifies the person and keep what informs the teaching. Combinations of details can still identify a learner (Rocher et al., 2019), so a small village together with a distinctive family detail can be enough.

TermWhat it means here
CapabilityWhat the learner knows, understands, can do, or can contribute. Start here so support does not erase strength.
BarrierA feature of the task, environment, representation, pacing, interaction or response mode that prevents the learner from showing capability.
AdjustmentA change to access or participation that reduces the barrier, such as visual scaffolds, structured routines, written plus verbal instructions, typing rather than handwriting, timers, checklists or a quiet space.
Learning expectationThe knowledge, reasoning or performance the learner is expected to demonstrate. An adjustment does not automatically lower this expectation.

Key principle. Change the pathway when the barrier is about access. Do not change the learning goal unless there is a separate professional basis for modifying the expected learning.

Access supports: AI, assistive technology and good design

Some accessibility supports use AI, some are established assistive technologies, and some are simply good instructional design. The question is not whether a support is AI-powered, but whether it removes an access barrier while preserving the learning.

Kooli and Chakraoui (2025) discuss AI-supported screen readers, voice assistants, speech recognition, language interfaces and gesture-based systems as potential accessibility supports. They also identify limits involving accuracy, infrastructure, educator readiness, privacy and bias.

A small Australian case study of three secondary students with disability found that the students made clear choices to use ChatGPT to learn, but long, dense answers and drifting topics made it hard to sustain the work without a teacher or education assistant. The authors recommend customising GenAI to learners’ specific needs, aligned with Universal Design for Learning, and clarifying the roles of teachers and assistants (Rappa et al., 2026).

Some of these supports use AI, some are established assistive technologies, and some are instructional design.
BarrierPossible supportHuman check
Visual access to digital textScreen reader, text-to-speech, structured alternative textDoes the tool accurately represent the content and preserve essential information?
Handwriting or fine-motor barrierSpeech-to-text, typing, voice commandsIs the learner still demonstrating the intended knowledge or reasoning?
Dense or complex languageStructured explanation, vocabulary support, contextual definitionsHas meaning been preserved, or has the task been simplified below the intended demand?
Multi-step instructionsChunking, visual sequence, checklist, reminder supportDoes the support reduce working-memory load without removing the thinking?
Communication or input barrierAlternative response mode, voice or multimodal responseIs the mode accessible and appropriate for the learner and context?

Five-step check. Barrier, support, learning goal, risk, human check. Use this sequence whenever AI or assistive technology is proposed as an adjustment.
