Numeracy is not simply calculation

How is numeracy currently understood in Indonesia, and what does this mean for the kinds of tasks we ask AI to generate?

In Module One you considered where AI’s view of the world comes from. As we turn our attention towards numeracy, it is important to acknowledge that this term is not used consistently across the world, with different countries and cultures affording it different meanings and adopting different approaches to when and how students learn it.

Consider the brief case studies below comparing Australia, Japan, and South Africa. As you read them, consider how they compare to the Indonesian context; if an AI system were to draw on information predominantly from one of the countries below, how appropriate is it likely to be for your teachers and students?

Each section includes links that will allow you to further explore numeracy in a country’s context; these are optional.

Australia

Australia treats numeracy as a capability that belongs to every teacher, not only teachers of mathematics. The Australian Curriculum describes students becoming numerate as they develop the knowledge and skills to use mathematics confidently across learning areas at school and in their lives more broadly (ACARA, n.d.-a). Numeracy therefore appears twice: as a general capability to be developed in every learning area, and through the separate Mathematics learning area. Its place in national policy was built up over 30 years through the Hobart (1989), Adelaide (1999) and Melbourne (2008) Declarations on the goals of schooling (Goos & O’Sullivan, 2023). In Version 9.0 the capability is expressed as a learning progression with three elements: number sense and algebra, measurement and geometry, and statistics and probability (ACARA, n.d.-a).

The Mathematics curriculum names four proficiencies (understanding, fluency, reasoning and problem-solving) and four mathematical processes, one of which is mathematical modelling. ACARA describes modelling as a five-step process (Figure 1). Current national guidance places it in an application phase, after the relevant knowledge and skills have been explicitly taught and practised (Leigh-Lancaster & Leigh-Lancaster, n.d.). The primary and secondary modelling content descriptions repeatedly specify financial contexts.

Two cautions come from the research. Goos and O’Sullivan (2023) argue that the relationship between numeracy and mathematics remains unclear in Australian policy. They note that the numeracy continuum could be used to support the Mathematics curriculum without engaging other learning areas at all. An analysis of Foundation–Year 6 content descriptions also found problem-solving and reasoning to be the least represented proficiencies (Reid O’Connor et al., 2023).

Explore further: Numeracy general capability · Modelling in the mathematics curriculum

Consider:

  • Numeracy here is everyone’s responsibility. How is that responsibility distributed in Indonesian curriculum documents and in your students’ schools?

  • The modelling tasks lean heavily on financial contexts. What would an AI system drawing mainly on this context assume about the “real-world” problems that matter to learners?

Japan

Japanese curriculum documents do not treat numeracy as a category. The concept appears as mathematical literacy, a term associated with PISA. It is infused into the mathematics curriculum itself, in contrast with Australia’s cross-curricular capability and South Africa’s stand-alone subject (Goos & O’Sullivan, 2023). The reform was prompted by concern that, despite strong rankings, results from TIMSS and PISA pointed to declining literacy performance and weak student interest and motivation in mathematics (Goos & O’Sullivan, 2023).

The vehicle is “mathematical activities”, part of the Course of Study since the 1998 revision. The 2008 guidelines emphasise three types: discovering and extending properties of numbers and figures; using mathematics in everyday life and society; and explaining and communicating using mathematical expressions. The guidelines state that, as a principle, these activities are carried out as problem solving. Listening to explanations or doing simple computation does not count as mathematical activity (Asami-Johansson, 2021).

In classrooms this is typically realised as a structured problem-solving lesson (Figure 2). The TIMSS video study found that Japanese lessons reverse the sequence common in the United States and Germany. Students attempt a problem first, then share the methods they generated, and the class works towards explicit understanding (Stigler et al., 1999). Teachers refine lessons collaboratively through Lesson Study (Asami-Johansson, 2021). The English-language guidance I could access reflects the 2008 guidelines. TIMSS reporting indicates the 2017 revision continues to stress applying mathematics in daily life and reflecting on the problem-solving process (Japan TIMSS Team, 2024).

Explore further: AAMT video of a Japanese structured problem-solving lesson (Grade 5, with expert commentary) · TIMSS Videotape Classroom Study (NCES) · Educational Studies in Mathematics article on structured problem-solving lessons

Consider:

  • Japan’s PISA-linked term was adopted from outside. Is numerasi a locally developed concept, or borrowed in a similar way, and does the origin matter for how it is taught?

  • No other teacher is asked to “teach numeracy” in this model. If an AI recommended Japanese-style lessons, what professional structures, such as shared lesson planning, would your teachers need to make them work?

South Africa

South Africa addresses numeracy through a stand-alone subject. Mathematical Literacy was introduced in 2006 for Grades 10–12 as an alternative to Mathematics, so that every learner continues to study some form of mathematics (Goos & O’Sullivan, 2023). The rationale is equity in post-apartheid reform. Between 2000 and 2005 as many as 40% of students took no mathematics at all (Volmink, 2018, as cited in Goos & O’Sullivan, 2023). The subject is framed as “mathematics for all”, not as a diluted course for weaker students (Goos & O’Sullivan, 2023).

The curriculum separates basic skills topics from application topics. Basic skills cover number, calculation, patterns, relationships and representations, and these are applied to finance, measurement, maps and plans, data handling and probability (Goos & O’Sullivan, 2023). Mathematical content is meant to be learned through real-life contexts. The curriculum policy says mathematical content makes sense of contexts, and contexts in turn determine the content needed (Department of Basic Education [DBE], 2011). Assessment follows a four-level taxonomy (Figure 3), with set proportions of marks at each level.

Research on enactment points to a gap between intention and practice. In a case study of four teachers, some found it hard to go beyond intra-mathematical problems, and the modelling process was not well understood (Botha & van Putten, 2018). Other concerns include limited teacher capacity, unequal school resources, and a risk that the subject is seen as lower in status than mathematics. Small classroom studies suggest it can also change learners’ mathematical identities and widen access (Goos & O’Sullivan, 2023; Graven & Buytenhuys, 2011).

Explore further: CAPS Mathematical Literacy, Grades 10–12 · Mathematical Literacy examination guidelines · Botha & van Putten (2018)

Consider:

  • The curriculum prescribes “authentic” contexts such as finance, maps and data. Who decides what counts as authentic for your learners, and how might the language of instruction affect their engagement with these contexts?

  • South Africa’s solution was driven by equity concerns. What does that suggest about whose numeracy needs an AI system trained mostly on other contexts might overlook?

Across the cases

The three countries place numeracy in three different positions: a capability spread across the curriculum (Australia), part of mathematics itself (Japan), and a subject in its own right (South Africa). Each position carries assumptions about who teaches numeracy, which contexts count, and how it is assessed. An AI system drawing mainly on one country would tend to reproduce that country’s assumptions, so it is worth checking each against your own teachers’ and students’ circumstances.

How AI handles numeracy questions

Currently, AI systems draw on a variety of approaches when solving numeracy problems. Common approaches are outlined in the table below:

Approach What happens Typical use
Direct generation Generates an answer directly from learned statistical patterns without deliberately invoking an external calculation tool. Quick or short questions where an external calculation is not required
Reasoning models Use additional intermediate processing before producing a final answer. Any visible explanation should not automatically be treated as a faithful record of the model’s internal process. Multi-step and word problems
Tool use (code or calculator) Invokes an external computation tool, such as code or a calculator, and uses the returned result in its response. Exact calculations, data analysis, verification and graphs
Retrieval/search Retrieves external information relevant to the problem rather than relying solely on information encoded in the model. Current information, formulas, definitions, curriculum documents and source verification

General users of AI may not be aware of which of the above methods are being used, and the method can vary between models, tasks, and runs. These approaches lead to several common limitations, some of which you would likely have seen in the example prompts.

Language models generate outputs by predicting tokens rather than operating as conventional calculators. In many models, multi-digit numbers are represented across one or more tokens, and the way those numbers are tokenised can affect arithmetic performance. AI outputs may give arithmetic errors when not deploying tools, with error rates rising with digit length (as in Prompt 1). Moreover, outputs are probabilistic, so the same prompt can take different reasoning paths, leading to inconsistent answers (try Prompt 1 again in a new chat - do you get the same answer as before?).

Some other common limitations are outlined below. These are observed patterns rather than fixed properties of every model, and the explanations offered are plausible mechanisms rather than definitive causes (see also Strohmaier et al., 2025; Turpin et al., 2023; Yen & Hsu, 2023):

Limitation Example Possible explanation
Place-value and decimal slips "9.11 > 9.9" (confusion with version or date notation) Numerical representations and tokenisation can affect arithmetic performance; surface patterns may sometimes compete with quantity relationships.
Surface-feature sensitivity Changing names or numbers, or adding irrelevant detail, changes accuracy Reliance on surface patterns can make performance sensitive to changes that do not alter the underlying mathematical structure.
Familiar-problem substitution Slightly altered classic puzzle is answered as the original Strong associations with familiar problem forms may influence responses to superficially similar problems.
Error accumulation Long multi-step problems drift off course An early error can propagate through later steps unless the model or surrounding system independently verifies the result.
Unfaithful explanations Correct answer with an invalid or inaccurate justification, or apparently plausible working that does not reliably reveal why the model produced its answer Chain-of-thought explanations can be plausible yet unfaithful to the factors that produced a model’s prediction (Turpin et al., 2023).
Weak contextual number sense Accepts an implausible answer, or overlooks units or real-world constraints Models do not automatically apply the contextual reasonableness checks expected of a numerate human. Contemporary models can still struggle when a word problem’s real-world context is problematic or non-sensical (Strohmaier et al., 2025).
Sycophancy Agrees with a user’s incorrect claim when challenged Optimisation for helpful, agreeable interaction can sometimes make a model overly responsive to a user’s stated position.
Missed tool use or tool misuse Attempts a calculation directly when an external tool would be safer, or produces faulty code or formulas Tool selection and tool-generated steps can themselves fail, so tool availability does not guarantee correct use.

Limitations also extend to the language used. MATH-IDN tested open-source models on mathematical problem solving in Indonesian, Javanese, Sundanese and Buginese and found substantial performance gaps for lower-resource languages, particularly Buginese (Xiao et al., 2026). PluraMath, a separate benchmark covering 18 underrepresented languages, likewise reports a persistent performance gap between high-resource and underrepresented languages (Hämmerl et al., 2026). Returning to the consideration of how numbers are represented and tokenised, in one study of multi-digit addition, inserting commas to enforce right-to-left digit grouping raised GPT-4 accuracy from 84.4% to 98.9% (Singh & Strouse, 2024). This demonstrates that seemingly superficial differences in numerical representation can materially affect model performance. This is particularly relevant in the Indonesian context, where conventions for the use of commas and decimal points differ from those used in many English-language contexts.

As previously noted, AI capabilities are evolving rapidly, so the limitations above are best treated as a snapshot rather than a fixed list. In OECD tests, GPT-3.5 answered 35% of a set of PISA mathematics tasks successfully in November 2022 and GPT-4 answered 40% in March 2023, compared with 51% for students (OECD, 2023). Later studies report much stronger performance. In Wei’s (2024) NAEP study, ChatGPT-4 and ChatGPT-4o generally outperformed U.S. students across Years 4, 8, and 12, although both models performed worse on geometry and measurement than on algebra. Strohmaier et al. (2025) reviewed 213 studies and also evaluated contemporary models on 287 word problems. The most recent models tested solved conventional word problems with near-perfect accuracy, including a perfect score on a set of 20 PISA problems, while still showing weaknesses when the real-world context made the straightforward mathematical solution problematic or nonsensical (such as failing to identify the unrealistic rice costs in Prompt 3). These findings illustrate why benchmark success should not automatically be interpreted as robust mathematical sense-making.

Using AI to assist with teaching, either directly as a tutor for students or indirectly through teacher guidance or resource development, raises additional considerations. Some relate to the AI’s general capabilities. For example, text-based models may generate weak or inaccurate representations when explaining concepts. Others concern mathematical and pedagogical capabilities: in early tests with GPT-3.5, models generated incorrect reasoning, misinterpreted questions and struggled to understand the rationale behind errors when correcting student answers (Yen & Hsu, 2023). More broadly, a recent systematic review of generative AI in mathematics education found both promising learning-support applications and recurring technical and pedagogical limitations, reinforcing the need for teacher judgement rather than assuming that fluent output is mathematically or pedagogically sound (Chao & Han, 2026). Models’ outputs are also shaped by patterns represented in their training data. Consequently, when used for teaching, they may reproduce inexact or potentially misleading mathematical language found in common usage (e.g. “borrowing”, “carrying” or “moving the decimal point”), or suggest pedagogical approaches that do not align with the conceptual or curriculum emphasis intended by the teacher. Purpose-built or locally controlled AI systems may address some context-specific needs, but their outputs still require evaluation against mathematical, curricular, and pedagogical goals.

Concept 2: Using generative AI for ideation

The limitations we have identified don’t mean that AI can’t be used to support the teaching of numeracy, but rather guide us on where we may need to provide additional context to the AI and where we may need to be particularly cautious when evaluating outputs.

A worked example

The example below is a copy of an M365 Copilot chat with an example prompt. Note that this prompt isn’t perfectly refined; we will discuss how we could improve prompts further (specifically in the context of generating numeracy tasks) in the live session.

Show the example prompt and the AI response

Prompt

Consider a Western Australian Year 7 Science class, currently studying Biological Sciences. Students are from the north metropolitan region. They have significant cultural diversity and academically range from A-E grades.

I want to provide students with a numeracy problem that they are likely to find relevant and meaningful, while aligning with curriculum content. Ideally, all students will be able to attempt the problem, but with different levels of sophistication. The problem should be open enough that students make their own choices regarding their approach and assumptions for the problem.

I will want students to align their overall approach to the ACARA mathematical modelling process (understand, plan, do, consider, communicate).

Suggest one appropriate numeracy problem.

Response

For what you are aiming for, I would make the problem local, ecological and decision-based, rather than turning the Science content into a disguised maths exercise.

The strongest curriculum connection is AC9S7U02: Year 7 students use models, including food webs, to represent matter and energy flow in ecosystems and predict the impact of changing abiotic and biotic factors on populations. That lends itself particularly well to an open mathematical modelling problem. [twinkl.com.au], [teachingto...kit.com.au]

Suggested problem: How much habitat should we keep?

The problem

A new housing development is being planned in Perth's northern suburbs.

The development site is 20 hectares. It currently contains native bushland that provides habitat for several species of plants and animals.

The developers want to use some of the land for housing, but the local community also wants enough bushland retained to support a healthy ecosystem.

Your challenge is to recommend how much of the 20-hectare site should be retained as habitat.

Use the information provided about the organisms living at the site and their relationships to develop a mathematical model that supports your recommendation.

There is no single correct answer. You will need to decide:

  • what information is important;

  • what assumptions you need to make;

  • how you will use mathematics to model the situation;

  • what you think should happen; and

  • how confident you are in your recommendation.

Your final recommendation needs to convince the local council that your proposed solution is reasonable.

I would then give students a relatively small ecological data pack, rather than putting all the mathematical decisions into the question itself.

For example, it might contain data on five or six plausible organisms:

  • native grasses/plants

  • insects

  • western grey kangaroos

  • bobtail lizards

  • birds

  • a predator

The data could include things such as estimated current population, habitat requirements, food relationships and perhaps observations from different parts of the site.

Why I think this is particularly strong

It naturally permits A–E differentiation without visibly giving different students different tasks.

A student at the lower end might reason:

"There are 20 ha. I think we should keep half, so 10 ha, because several animals depend on the bushland."

They can draw the food web, calculate simple fractions or percentages, and make a defensible recommendation.

A middle-achieving student might calculate:

"If 12 ha is retained, that's 60% of the original habitat. If we assume populations decrease approximately in proportion to available habitat, the estimated kangaroo population would decrease from 15 to about 9."

A sophisticated student might challenge that assumption altogether:

"Population probably isn't directly proportional to area because a single connected 10 ha habitat may support species better than two isolated 5 ha areas. Therefore, my model considers both total area and habitat fragmentation."

That is exactly the kind of increasing sophistication of modelling, rather than simply increasing difficulty of arithmetic, that I think would suit your purpose.

Connection to the modelling process

I would explicitly organise the student response around the five stages you mentioned:

UNDERSTAND
What is the problem? What do we know about the ecosystem? What information matters?

↓

PLAN
What mathematics could help? What assumptions will I make? What information will I use?

↓

DO
Calculate, estimate, construct tables/graphs, develop a food-web or population model, compare possible amounts of habitat retained.

↓

CONSIDER
Does my answer make sense scientifically? What would happen to different organisms? How realistic were my assumptions? What doesn't my model account for?

↓

COMMUNICATE
Recommend an amount of habitat to retain and use mathematical and scientific evidence to justify it.

The Consider stage is particularly valuable here. A mathematically neat answer could be biologically implausible, which forces students to bring the Science back into their mathematical reasoning.

The important design choice

I would not give students a formula such as:

population remaining = original population × percentage of habitat retained.

That would substantially weaken the task because you've already made the modelling decision for them.

Instead, give them the raw information and let them decide how habitat area might relate to population. If some students decide to assume a proportional relationship, that's an entirely legitimate Year 7 model. More capable students can recognise its limitations.

Likewise, I wouldn't ask a sequence such as "calculate 60% of 20 ha; then calculate..." The question should remain genuinely:

How much habitat should we keep, and why?

with the mathematics emerging because students need it.

Why it fits Year 7 Science rather than merely being "numeracy in Science"

The Year 7 curriculum expects students to represent matter and energy flows in ecosystems and predict effects of environmental changes. The Year 7 achievement standard similarly expects students to represent flows through ecosystems and predict effects of environmental change. [teachingto...kit.com.au], [twinkl.com.au] [biancacarr...weebly.com]

So reducing available habitat isn't just the backdrop for an otherwise mathematical problem. The biological consequence of changing an ecosystem is actually what students are modelling.

I also like the Perth northern-suburbs context because students don't need specialist cultural knowledge, home ownership experience or particular leisure interests to enter the problem. "Bushland versus development" is concrete, observable and locally meaningful, while the decision itself has enough ambiguity that students can reasonably arrive at different answers.

Overall, this is the one I'd choose: it has a very low floor, a genuinely high ceiling, obvious Biological Sciences alignment, meaningful opportunities for percentages/ratio/measurement/data representation, and most importantly, it doesn't tell students what mathematics to use.

A brief evaluation might note: While the task does align strongly with the Western Australian curriculum, the AI has actually drawn from and referenced the ACARA curriculum, which is not the same as the Western Australian curriculum (in Australia, all states/territories are responsible for setting their own curriculum). Note that the AI’s sources seem to be Twinkl, a teaching-toolkit site and a teacher’s personal Weebly page. The task has strong relevance in the local context, noting that this does still vary greatly from school to school within this region, and that since this is not a scenario arising in students’ everyday lives, many students may perceive less personal relevance, particularly given their young age. There are significant opportunities for students to engage with the problem in diverse ways, through making assumptions, engaging in research, investigating and representing relevant mathematical and scientific content. This is somewhat limited by students’ capabilities and awareness of appropriate strategies at this stage of their learning; it would likely require significant prompting/scaffolding. While the AI suggests approaches that would be within the assumed knowledge of a Year 7 student, the lower end response doesn’t meaningfully engage with the task at all and the middle-achieving response does not reflect the reality of the population-area relationship (as noted by the AI). Many students are likely to be overwhelmed by the task, given that they would not be aware of the relevant mathematics that actually models the scenario. The approaches that would lie within students’ knowledge are very limited. The data pack may provide more viable opportunities. The burden for evaluating the trade-off between housing and species preservation is also too significant for students in the current presentation of the task. Overall, this does present a potentially interesting task that could be appropriate with substantial modifications. The closest implementation to the current proposal might involve students collectively discussing the problem (what they are trying to do, what assumptions they might need to make, what approaches they might take, further information they might need, and any limitations). Students might then choose between options of different complexity to model the scenario and evaluate their findings, including a meaningful discussion in the context of their scientific knowledge and understanding, as well as discussing limitations and appropriateness. This could end in a showcase of different groups’ approaches to the task.
