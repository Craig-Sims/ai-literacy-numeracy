Start with an assessment you already use

Assessment starts with a question that existed long before generative AI: what do you need to know about what a learner understands or can do?

Think about one assessment, practicum task, lesson-planning task, professional reflection or other assessed activity that you currently use with PPG students. It does not need to be a formal examination. Choose something that gives you evidence about their knowledge, reasoning or professional practice.

Now imagine that a capable generative AI system could produce a convincing final submission for that task in a few minutes. That does not automatically make the assessment invalid. It does, however, force us to ask what evidence of learning the task currently makes visible, and what might disappear if we only look at the final product.

Why assessment design matters in an AI-aware environment

Generative AI has changed the conditions under which students complete learning tasks. It has not changed the core purposes of assessment. Assessment still needs to make learning visible, provide evidence against intended outcomes, inform teaching, and support learners to improve.

Early responses to generative AI often focused on detecting whether a final product had been produced by AI. Detection does not solve the underlying assessment problem. Even if a detector were perfectly accurate, it would not tell us whether the task itself gives strong evidence of learning. A more defensible response is to design assessment so that reasoning, process and decision-making become visible.

Research increasingly describes a move away from product-only assessment toward assessment that includes process evidence, staged decisions, explanation and critique. This does not mean every assessment must use AI. It means the role of AI, if any, should be determined by the learning intention rather than left implicit (Nguyen et al., 2025; Weng et al., 2024).

Concept 1: What are you actually assessing?

Diagnose your task before learning the framework

Return to the assessment you identified in Ground. Do not use AI yet. Work through the four questions below using your own professional judgement.

Classify the current task. Choose the description that best fits your task now:

  • AI-fragile - a convincing final product could be produced with little meaningful evidence of the student's own reasoning.
  • AI-resistant - the task includes contextual, process or performance evidence that makes the learner's own thinking more visible.
  • AI-integrated - AI use is deliberately built into the task and the assessment evaluates how the learner uses, critiques or makes decisions with it.

Make learning visible, not merely the final product

A final product can be useful evidence, but it is rarely the whole story. In an AI-aware environment, assessment becomes more defensible when it also reveals how the learner arrived at the product, what decisions they made, and whether they can explain or apply the underlying knowledge.

This can be done without turning every assessment into a surveillance exercise. Staged drafting, brief process commentaries, explanation of choices, oral or live components, application to a supplied context, structured reflection, critique of alternatives and visible revision can all provide evidence of thinking. These are established assessment practices; generative AI simply increases the importance of using them deliberately.

The key question is therefore not, "Did AI touch this task?" The stronger question is, "Does this assessment still provide valid evidence of the intended learning?"

Nguyen et al. (2025) describe this as a shift from product-only evaluation toward product-process assessment. Weng et al. (2024) similarly identify traditional, refocused and AI-incorporated approaches to assessment. The practical implication is that teachers and teacher educators can choose different assessment designs for different purposes rather than imposing one rule across every task.

Return to your diagnosis. Name one specific vulnerability in your current assessment. What important evidence of learning is currently weak, invisible or too easy to replace with a polished AI-generated product?

Redesign before asking AI

Choose one or two design moves:

Your redesign. Write the revised instruction or task requirement exactly as you would give it to your PPG students. Then answer: What new evidence of learning would this change make visible?

Make the permitted role of AI explicit

An assessment does not need a single rule about AI from beginning to end. The appropriate role of AI can differ across stages of the task. In some stages, no AI may be appropriate because independent recall or performance is the evidence being assessed. In others, limited AI support may be useful. In still others, the ability to use and critique AI may itself be part of the learning.

Think of AI use as a design decision rather than a progression toward "more AI". The correct position is the one that best protects the intended learning.

PositionWhat it means
No AIThe task or stage requires independent demonstration of knowledge or performance.
AI for preparationAI may support planning, idea generation or resource finding, but not produce assessed work.
AI-supportedAI may support a defined part of the process, such as language refinement, questions or feedback.
AI-integratedAI use is deliberately built into the assessed task and students must explain or justify how they used it.
AI as object of critiqueStudents analyse, verify, correct or improve AI output as part of the disciplinary learning.

AI can support feedback, but it does not become the assessor

AI can be useful in formative assessment because it can rapidly generate question stems, alternative explanations, possible misconceptions, exemplars for critique and suggested next steps. Used in this way, AI can widen the pool of possibilities available to the educator.

The boundary is evaluative authority. Assigning achievement, grading work, deciding progression and making consequential judgements about learners require contextual professional judgement. The source material for this programme therefore positions AI as a planning and feedback assistant rather than as the assessor.

Ilieva et al. (2025) similarly emphasise structured human oversight in AI-supported assessment. The practical principle for this programme is simple: AI may suggest; the educator evaluates.

This also changes what mastery looks like. Disciplinary knowledge remains essential. Students need enough subject knowledge to recognise when AI output is wrong, shallow or inappropriate. AI literacy adds to disciplinary mastery; it does not replace it.
